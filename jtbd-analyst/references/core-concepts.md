# Core Concepts of JTBD

> Distilled from Jim Kalbach, *The Jobs to Be Done Playbook* (Rosenfeld Media, 2020), Ch. 1–2. See `sources.md` for attribution.

## What a "job" is

A job is **the process of reaching an objective under a given set of circumstances** — a goal that exists independently of any product or solution. People don't buy products because of who they are demographically; they "hire" solutions to make progress on a job. The classic framing: people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole (Levitt). The drill is a means; the hole is the job.

JTBD is a **lens, not a single method**. It deliberately avoids naming solutions so you can understand the underlying intent first, then design to it.

## Two schools of thought (both useful)

- **Switch (Bob Moesta):** qualitative interviews that reverse-engineer why someone changed from one solution to another. Goal: increase demand. Best when you start with an existing product in mind. → see `four-forces.md`, `interview-guides.md`.
- **Outcome-Driven Innovation / ODI (Tony Ulwick, Strategyn):** uncovers every desired outcome for a job, then prioritizes them with a quantitative survey. Goal: increase adoption by targeting unmet needs. Best for new products or redefining a market. → see `needs-and-opportunities.md`.

They are not mutually exclusive. Use ODI bottom-up when defining a market; use Switch when you already have a product and want to know why people hire it.

## The five core elements

| Element | Question | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Job performer** | Who? | The end user who actually executes the main job |
| **Job** | What? | The objective the performer wants to accomplish |
| **Process** | How? | The stages they move through to get the job done |
| **Needs** | Why? | The performer's requirements / desired outcomes during the job |
| **Circumstances** | When/where? | Contextual factors that frame execution |

## The job ecosystem: separate the roles

Distinguish the **job performer** from everyone else. The performer's needs come first; design for them, then consider the others. These are functional roles ("hats"), not job titles — one person can wear several (common in B2C); in B2B they're usually different people.

- **Performer** — executes the job (primary focus)
- **Buyer** — purchases the solution
- **Approver** — authorizes the purchase (controller, budget holder, parent)
- **Reviewer** — checks appropriateness (lawyer, compliance, consultant)
- **Technician** — installs / integrates / gets it working (IT, installer)
- **Manager** — oversees the performer doing the job
- **Audience** — consumes the output of the job (client, downstream team)
- **Assistant** — helps the performer get the job done

> Sequence of attention: **performer → buyer → everyone else.** Buyer needs matter, but the solution must serve the performer first.

## Types of jobs

- **Main job** — the overall functional aim; sets the scope of innovation. Stable, solution-free, has a clear "done" state. e.g., *prepare a meal*, *plan long-term financial well-being*. No adjectives (quick/easy/cheap are *needs*, handled separately).
- **Related jobs** — adjacent but distinct objectives that clarify what the main job is and isn't (and sometimes compete with it). e.g., main *grow retirement portfolio* → related *finance a new home*, *balance cash flow*.
- **Emotional jobs** — how the performer wants to *feel*. Usually start with "feel…" (e.g., *feel safe at home*).
- **Social jobs** — how the performer wants to be *perceived* by others (e.g., *avoid embarrassment in public*).

> **Sequence for innovation:** solve the functional job first, then layer emotional and social jobs. Even in emotion-heavy domains (e.g., fashion), the blocking unmet need is often functional (e.g., *does it fit?*).

## Formulating job statements

**Pattern: verb + object + (clarifier)**

Examples: *visit family on special occasions*; *remove snow from pathways*; *listen to music on a run*; *plan long-term financial well-being*.

Trick: imagine a silent "I want to…" in front of each statement, then drop it. Add "e.g., …" examples after the statement for clarity.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Reflect the individual's perspective | Refer to technology or solutions |
| Start with a verb | Reference methods or techniques |
| Ensure stability over time | Reflect observations or preferences |
| Clarify with context if needed | Use compound concepts (no AND / OR) |

## Hierarchy and scope

Jobs nest: a higher-level job has sub-jobs; sub-jobs have steps. When scoping a project, pick the level of the **main job** deliberately — too broad and it's unactionable, too narrow and it bakes in today's solution. Use related jobs to find the right altitude.
